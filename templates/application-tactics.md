# Application Form Tactics

Recurring application-form questions and how to handle each. Conservative, ATS-aware, integrity-first.

---

## "If you heard about us through a referral, please state the [Company] employee's name."

| Scenario | What to put |
|---|---|
| **Real referral** (alum said "yes, I'll refer you") | Their full name, coordinated with them so the ATS match works. |
| **Messaged alum, no response yet** | Leave blank or "N/A". Don't name them without permission. |
| **Talked to alum but no explicit referral consent** | Leave blank. In "How did you hear" use: *"Found via [source]; also in conversation with [Name] about [Company]"*, honest, doesn't claim referral. |
| **Casual friend tip, no formal referral** | Leave blank. |
| **No connection** | Leave blank. Use "Direct portal scan" or job board name in "How did you hear" instead. |

**Why not fabricate:**
1. ATSes route referral-tagged apps to different/faster reviewer queues. Mismatch flags the application.
2. Referral bonuses create paperwork problems for wrongly-named employees.
3. Recruiters sometimes verify. Fabricating gets you blacklisted at the company AND its sister companies (recruiters share notes).

## "How did you hear about this role?"

Honest, brief options ranked by quality:

1. **Direct portal scan via Greenhouse / Ashby / Lever API** (technical, signals you ran a structured search; recruiters appreciate this)
2. **CMU Alumni Online Community / Tartan Connect**
3. **LinkedIn job search**
4. **Handshake (CMU)**
5. **SimplifyJobs / Pitt CSC / cvrve / ouckah** (curated new-grad boards)
6. **YC Work at a Startup** (for YC-backed companies)
7. **A specific blog post or conference talk** (e.g., Cloudflare's Agents Week 2026)

Avoid: "Saw on social media" (too vague), "Friend told me" (without naming the friend it sounds made up).

## "Are you authorized to work in the US?" / "Will you require sponsorship?" (Yes/No or dropdown)

Answer truthfully but minimally:

- **"Authorized to work?"** → **Yes** (OPT work authorization after December 2026 graduation; 12 months OPT plus the 24-month STEM extension).
- **"Will you now or in the future require sponsorship?"** → **Yes** (H-1B sponsorship will be needed in the future). If the option "Yes, in the future" exists, pick it; it is the most accurate single answer.
- **"Are you a U.S. citizen or permanent resident?"** → **No.** (No further explainer. If a free-text "comments" box appears, leave it blank.)

**Do NOT volunteer the full visa explainer paragraph** unless explicitly asked. Most application forms don't have a "tell us about your visa" field, and adding unsolicited visa context to a "Why this role?" answer is a known anti-pattern.

## "What is your expected salary?"

New-grad full-time roles are quoted as an annual base salary. Treat these as guidance bands, not a number you must commit to on the form.

If the field has a dropdown or salary band:
- Pick the band you'd be happy with, not the highest possible.
- For new-grad SDE / ML / DS at top-tier US companies: a base in the **$140K-180K** band is a reasonable mid-market ask (total comp runs higher with equity and bonus).

If the field is a single number:
- For SDE / ML / DS new-grad at FAANG / top AI labs: base around **$160K-180K**.
- For mid-tier US tech: base around **$130K-150K**.
- For early-stage AI startups: base around **$120K-150K plus equity**.
- For CMU spinoffs / Pittsburgh-local: base around **$110K-130K**.

If the field is "any" or text: write **"Open to standard new-grad base for this role and location"** to avoid pre-anchoring.

**Don't lowball.** Recruiters respect candidates who know their market value. Lowballing makes you look desperate or junior.

## "Why are you interested in this role?" (free text, ≤300 words)

The 4-part formula:

1. **Specific reason for THIS role at THIS company** (1-2 sentences, citing something concrete from JD or company materials)
2. **Most relevant project from your work** (2-3 sentences, with a metric or specific technical detail)
3. **What you'd want to build / contribute** (1-2 sentences, ideally referencing a specific product or feature)
4. **Availability** (1 sentence, only if the field asks: "Available January 2027")

What NOT to do:
- Don't open with "I'm a CMU MISM master's student". Resume already says this.
- Don't say "passionate about" / "leveraged" / "results-oriented".
- Don't include the visa/sponsorship paragraph.

- **Default**: none to volunteer. If a start date is asked, "Available January 2027" stands alone.

## General principles

1. **Truth first**. ATSes and recruiters cross-check, and the cost of being caught lying once is permanent at that company.
2. **Brevity**. Most form fields reward concise answers. 200 words is a lot. See [Answer length](#answer-length-the-standard) for the hard numbers.
3. **Specificity**. Recruiters skim hundreds of apps; specific details (project names, metrics, specific products you want to work on) signal a real candidate.
4. **No boilerplate**. If you wouldn't write a phrase to a friend, don't write it on the form. "Passionate about" / "results-driven" / "leveraged" are dead giveaways.
5. **Match the company's voice**. Stripe (terse, direct). Airbnb (story-driven, values-led). Cloudflare (AI-native, builder-energy). Brex (production-fintech, bottom-line). Sierra (high-formality, founder-pedigree).

## Answer length: the standard

Researched 2026-07-27 after a drafted answer ran 357 words on a no-limit form. Sources at the bottom of this section. Default to these numbers; do not re-litigate per question.

### Target by field type

| Situation | Target | Ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| **Stated word limit** (e.g. "max 300 words") | ~15% under the max (300 → **~255**) | Never exceed. Over-limit is a screening risk, not a flourish. |
| **No limit stated, large text box / Google Forms long-answer** | **150-250 words** | 300. Past that you are writing for yourself. |
| **No limit stated, single-line input** | 1-2 sentences | 40 words |
| **Character-capped field** (LinkedIn-style) | Count it before drafting | Hard cap |
| **Behavioral / STAR answer, written** | **150-200 words** | 250 |
| **Cover-letter STAR story (inside a letter)** | One paragraph, 4-5 sentences | See the 200-word body cap in `templates/cover-letter.md` |

### Rules

1. **Multi-part questions partition the budget, they do not multiply it.** A question asking four things ("make the case, name a real decision, what it cost, what it bought") still gets ~200 words total, roughly a sentence or two per part. Answer every part explicitly, because a missing part reads as evasion, but do not give each part a paragraph. This is the exact error that triggered this section.
2. **Brevity is a differentiator, not a shortfall.** Reviewers read a very high volume of these. An answer notable for saying the thing and stopping stands out; there is no penalty for finishing under a stated limit. Padding to reach a limit reads as waffle and is a negative signal.
3. **But do not under-fill a large box.** Box size is the only length signal a form gives you. Fill it or about three-quarters of it. Two or three sentences in a full-page textarea looks like low effort. When the box is a Google Forms long-answer field (no size signal at all), fall back to the 150-250 band.
4. **Spend the words on what you did.** Rough STAR allocation: ~10% situation, ~10% task, **~60% action**, ~20% result. Diagnostic: if the setup and the thesis together outrun what you actually did, rebalance rather than trim the ending.
5. **One length, not two.** Draft a single answer at the right length. Shipping a long version plus a short variant is deferring the decision to the user, and the variants then drift out of sync.
6. **Cut restatement first.** The reviewer just read the question. Re-narrating it is the cheapest 40 words to delete, followed by scene-setting and any sentence that only announces what the next sentence will say.

### When longer is actually defensible

Only two cases, and both need evidence from the posting itself:

- The form **states** a higher limit (500+ words), or the box is explicitly a writing sample.
- The JD screens on written communication (Eulerity's does: "clear technical documentation"). Even then the answer earns length through density, not volume. Ceiling stays 300.

A JD merely being senior, technical, or interesting is not a reason to write more.

**Sources:** [Claire Jutsum, writing to the word count](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/how-approach-writing-your-application-within-word-count-claire-jutsum) (~15% under a stated limit) · [AllAboutSchoolLeavers, application mistakes](https://www.allaboutschoolleavers.co.uk/school-leaver-options/apprenticeships/application-mistakes-to-avoid) (fill the box or three-quarters; blank space and waffle both hurt) · [Probably Good, application questions](https://probablygood.org/how-to-get-a-job/application-questions/) (shorter answers are easier to read and remember; add insight rather than padding) · [The Interview Guys, STAR method](https://blog.theinterviewguys.com/the-star-method/) (150-200 words written; 10/10/60/20 allocation) · [DC OCFO, supplemental questions guide](https://cfo.dc.gov/page/application-guide-responding-supplemental-questions) (3-4 developed sentences answers most prompts) · [StandOut CV, recruiter reading-time study 2026](https://standout-cv.com/stats/how-long-recruiters-spend-looking-at-cv) (skim behavior under volume)

## AI-lab-specific form patterns

In scope: Anthropic, OpenAI, Cohere, Hippocratic AI, Mistral, Perplexity, Adobe Firefly / Applied Research, Match Group AI/ML, Cerebras, plus any frontier-lab applied or research role. These forms tend to use four recurring prompts, in some combination. Use the templates below; pick the project anchor that matches the lab's stated work area.

### Prompt 1: "What makes you a good fit?"

Formula: three intersections, one sentence each, no fluff. Pick the three from {production engineering, applied ML, multi-agent LLM, research depth, healthcare data, hackathon shipping}. Lead with whichever the lab cares about most based on the JD.

```
Three intersections most candidates don't have at once. One: shipped a real
multi-agent LLM system (Cloudify on OpenAI plus Anthropic Claude through the
Dedalus SDK, full-stack cloud migration days to under 20 minutes across 8+
stack configurations). Two: 2.5 years production SDE at Byju's (200K+ DAU
e-commerce, $400K AWS to GCP migration, 100% test coverage on a Scheduling
microservice). Three: applied ML on real data (Highmark cancer-staging
XGBoost on 6M+ longitudinal claims records, addressing 40 to 51% staging-
field missingness with NCCN-augmented features). [LAB] cares about all
three; doing them at once is exactly where I want to grow.
```

**EEG-anchored template (best for research-oriented roles, Anthropic Frontiers, OpenAI Research, Cerebras Applied, Adobe Applied Scientist, Apple ML Research):**

```
Three intersections. One: hands-on neural-architecture work at scale (CMU
```

### Prompt 3: "What do you want to learn this summer?"

Concrete, not fluffy. Tie to a specific lab paper, product, or team if known; otherwise tie to a specific technical area. Avoid "the cutting edge" and "your incredible work."

**Template (when you know one specific work product):**

```
Two specific things. One: how [LAB] handles [SPECIFIC_TECHNICAL_PROBLEM,
e.g., "agent memory eviction at production scale" / "speculative decoding
on Wafer-Scale" / "RLHF reward-model drift on long-context tasks"]. The
trade-off I keep hitting in [Cloudify / Highmark / EEG] is [SPECIFIC_TRADE-
OFF], and [LAB]'s [PAPER / PRODUCT] is the productized version of what I
hand-rolled. Two: how a team [LAB-size] coordinates the engineering vs
research split when shipping an evolving system. My instinct from Byju's
production work is that the boundary is more porous than most orgs admit;
I want to see how [LAB] draws it.
```

**Template (when you don't have a specific paper to cite):**

```
Two things. One: [SPECIFIC TECHNICAL AREA, e.g., "production eval
infrastructure for multi-agent systems" / "long-context retrieval on noisy
domain data" / "deployment of small-model fine-tunes alongside frontier
APIs"]. I have started touching this in [Cloudify / Highmark], but my
current solutions are hand-rolled, and I want to see how a team that does
this at scale builds the abstractions. Two: code-review and shipping
discipline at a research-heavy team. My production background is at Byju's,
which is engineering-led; I want to see how a research-led org keeps
shipping rigor.
```

### Prompt 4: "Why this lab over Lab X?"

Frame around alignment of research direction or product space, not flattery. Never disparage the comparison lab. The honest move is to pick one specific axis where the target lab's bet differs and say why that bet matches your work.

**Template:**

```
[LAB] and [COMPARISON_LAB] are both serious. The reason I'm specifically
applying here is [SPECIFIC AXIS: e.g., "your bet on enterprise developer
workflows over consumer chat" / "the post-training research line published
in [PAPER]" / "your willingness to ship Wafer-Scale to enterprise customers
rather than position as research-only" / "the agentic-platform direction
the Agents Week 2026 announcements signal"]. That axis matches the work
I'm doing now in [Cloudify / Highmark / EEG], and the kind of summer I
want is one where the lab's product or research direction and my project
trajectory point the same way.
```

### Recurring patterns to AVOID across all four companies

- Lifting the value's exact wording back at the form ("I am passionate about being a Cereal Entrepreneur"). Map the behavior, not the noun.
- Picking the value that sounds best instead of the value the story actually proves. If Anmol's story is about edge-case rigor, "Be a Host" or "Technical clarity" is honest; "Champion the Mission" is fake.
- Stretching one story across all four values. Use four different stories if the form asks for four.

## Case-study / take-home prompt handling

Some companies (often deployment-engineering, applied-AI, infra) ship a take-home rather than a phone screen. The default failure mode is over-scoping; the right move is to ship a small, honest, end-to-end thing.

### Time-boxing rules

- **A 4-hour take-home should ship in ~3.5 hours of build, with the last 30 minutes spent on the README and the "what I'd do with more time" appendix.** The appendix is what proves you scoped intentionally rather than ran out of time.
- **A 24-hour take-home should ship in 18 hours of build, with 4 hours of polish (README, edge cases, tradeoffs writeup) and 2 hours of buffer.**
- **A 1-week take-home should ship in 4 working days of build + 1 day of writeup. Reserve the buffer days; do not consume them.**
- If the prompt says "should take ~4 hours" and you have spent 6, stop building, write the README, ship what you have. Honesty beats over-delivery.

### Scope-narrowing

When a prompt is open-ended (e.g., "build a small system that does X"), pick the smallest end-to-end thing.

- End-to-end beats partial-but-deeper. A working CLI that does one thing badly is better than a half-built API that does three things.
- Pick the narrowest interpretation of the prompt, ship it, and use the README to enumerate what a fuller interpretation would change. Reviewers can extrapolate from a working narrow thing; they cannot extrapolate from a broken broad thing.
- If the prompt is ambiguous, write down the assumption you made in the README (one bullet, top of "Assumptions" section) before you start coding. The assumption itself is signal.

### Deliverable structure

Every take-home repo should ship with these five sections in the README, in order:

1. **What this is** (one paragraph: what was asked, what was built)
2. **Run instructions** (exact commands; tested on a fresh machine if possible)
3. **Assumptions** (bulleted list; what you decided to interpret the prompt as)
4. **Tradeoffs** (bulleted list; what you chose vs alternatives, and why)
5. **What I would do with more time** (bulleted list; ordered by impact, not effort)

Optional sixth section if relevant: **Architecture notes** (one diagram or one paragraph; only if the system is non-trivial).

Code organization: one entry point, named runnable. Tests if the prompt mentions correctness. Type hints if Python. A `requirements.txt` or `package.json` even for tiny things.

### AI-tool disclosure norm in the artifact

- **Default**: do not proactively mention Claude / Copilot / Cursor use in the README or the cover note.
- **If asked**: be honest. "Used Claude for [SPECIFIC: e.g., scaffolding the test harness, writing the README first draft, debugging a regex, exploring API design alternatives]; the architecture decisions and tradeoff calls were mine."
- **Never**: claim no AI use if you used AI. Detection is improving, and AI-lab recruiters specifically cross-check.
- **AI-lab forms** sometimes ask explicitly: "Did you use AI tools to prepare this submission?" Always say yes if you did, and itemize what for.

### "Are you willing to work on-site / in-office?" (when JD is in-office only)

```
Yes, willing to work on-site at [OFFICE NAMED IN JD].
```

### "When can you start?" / "Earliest start date?"

```
Available January 2027.
```

Do not add any OPT / sponsorship context or "pending visa logistics." The January 2027 line stands alone.

### "Are you authorized to work in the US?"

```
Yes.
```

No further explainer. Work authorization comes through OPT after the December 2026 graduation, but the form answer is simply "Yes." Do not volunteer the visa mechanism unless a sponsorship-specific field asks.

### "Do you require sponsorship now or in the future?"

This question IS sponsorship-relevant; it is not the same as the visa-explainer. Answer honestly.

```
Yes, I will require H-1B sponsorship in the future.
```

If the form is a single dropdown with one answer:

- **Pick "Yes, in the future"** if that option exists. It is the most accurate single answer.
- If only "Yes / No" with no temporal qualifier, **pick "Yes"**. Lying on a binary sponsorship question is the highest-cost form-error category; recruiters cross-check at offer time and pulled offers over this are documented.

### "Did you use AI tools to prepare this application?"

Be honest. Most AI labs specifically ask this as a calibration question; the honest answer is also the right answer.

```
Yes. I used Claude to help structure my responses; the experience and judgment in the content are mine.
```

If the form provides a free-text explanation field:

```
Yes. I used Claude (Anthropic) to help with: (1) structuring multi-paragraph
responses, (2) tightening word counts on free-text fields, (3) cross-
referencing my application against my own playbook of recurring form-question
patterns. Decisions about which projects to anchor, which tradeoffs to
surface, and which company-specific details to cite are mine.
```

### What NOT to do on any of the above

- Do not volunteer F-1 / OPT / STEM-extension / I-20 / SEVIS / Heinz / OIE in any answer.
- Do not say "available pending visa logistics" or "subject to OPT processing." January 2027 stands alone.
- Do not lie about AI tool use on a form that asked.
- Do not fake a permanent address near a target office to look "local." Pittsburgh through Dec 2026 is the honest answer.
- Do not promise to relocate to a country you cannot get a visa for (e.g., Toronto for a Canadian-HQ company without a Canadian work permit). Pre-empt this in the cover letter (see Cohere precedent in `reports/cohere/002-cohere-cover-letter.md`).
